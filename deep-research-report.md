## Pricing, policies, and booking strategy

You asked for the website to show costs, but this is the one area where the site must be highly disciplined. Airbnb hides exact pricing behind date entry, and its page explicitly asks users to add travel dates to know the exact price. Vrbo also shows dynamic pricing based on selected dates, and on July 28, 2026 it surfaced a sample price of at least **€3,906** for a one-night stay for two adults on a future October date, which is not useful as a premium headline because it is date-specific and platform-calculated. In other words, the marketplace pages do **not** provide a stable, public, brand-safe rate card for the main villa. citeturn3view0turn5view0

The right solution is to present a **curated seasonal rate table** that you control outside the booking platforms. Agents should request from you an owner-approved pricing sheet with, at minimum, the following rows: **Low Season**, **Shoulder Season**, **High Season**, and **Peak Weeks**, each broken into **Villa Only** and **Villa + Pool Cottage**. Until that sheet exists, the site should not publish scraped or inferred nightly rates for the main villa. Instead, it can say “Seasonal weekly rates available” and direct guests to the booking platforms for final totals. That will keep the site elegant and reduce mismatch risk. The one rate figure that can already be published with confidence is the **weekly supplement for the pool cottage**, which Airbnb gives as **€780 to €1,350 plus €120 cleaning and linen**, while Vrbo gives **€750 to €1,490 plus €120 cleaning and linen**. Because the two platforms differ slightly, the site should display this as an **indicative range** and note that final pricing depends on platform and dates. citeturn2view0turn5view0

The site should clearly communicate the **minimum stay logic**. Airbnb states that the minimum stay is **7 days** and even notes that available dates appear when Sunday is selected as the start of stay. That is operationally important and should be turned into a refined line such as: “Weekly stays are the standard rhythm of the house; peak-season arrival patterns may follow Sunday turnover.” This preserves the upscale tone while still reflecting the listing’s constraints. citeturn2view0

Policies belong on the website, but they should live in an elegant FAQ rather than in the hero area. The key policy facts currently visible on the platform pages are: **check-in 16:00–19:00 on Airbnb and from 16:30 on Vrbo; check-out by 10:00; no pets; minimum renter age 28 on Vrbo; no events; smoking only in designated areas on Vrbo; children allowed but with site-specific caution due terraces and elevated areas; pool open from May to October on Airbnb**. Airbnb also states that, because the home is a tourist rental and not a B&B, extra cleaning, linen changes, and consumable goods are not provided during the stay in the way they would be at a serviced property. This is exactly the type of information that prevents bad-fit guests while increasing trust among the right ones. citeturn2view0turn3view2turn6view2

One more policy area needs special handling: **tourist tax**. The platform pages conflict. Airbnb says **€2.50 per night per guest over 15 up to the tenth night**; Vrbo shows a house-rule note mentioning **€1.50 per night per guest over 15**; but Camogli’s official 2026 tourist-tax portal states that updated tariffs apply from 2026 and its published tariff table shows **€3.00** per person per night for both “locazioni brevi” and “appartamento ammobiliato ad uso turistico.” Because the official local table and the platform pages do not align, the site should not hardcode a single tax number as if it were definitive. The correct website treatment is: **“Tourist tax may apply under current Camogli municipal rules and is confirmed on the booking platform at time of reservation.”** citeturn3view2turn7view4turn8search1turn9view0

## Information architecture and content choreography

For a single extraordinary property, the strongest move is a **luxury long-form homepage** with anchored sections rather than a conventional multi-page rental site. Guests should feel carried through a sequence. The information hierarchy should be emotional first, rational second.

The page should open with a **full-viewport cinematic hero** using one stunning still image or an ultra-short silent loop approved by you. The copy should avoid marketplace language. A strong headline would be something like: **“A private Ligurian villa above Camogli.”** The subline should compress the offer into one elegant sentence: sea-view hillside privacy, terraced garden, pool, proximity to Camogli and Portofino. The primary CTAs in the hero should be **“View on Airbnb”** and **“View on Vrbo”**, both opening new tabs. A tertiary in-page link such as **“Explore the villa”** can scroll users downward. That preserves the no-direct-booking rule while still feeling premium. The need to keep booking on reputable platforms is supported both by Airbnb’s payment guidance on the listing and by BBB scam-prevention advice. citeturn3view2turn1search16

## GitHub Pages deployment blueprint

GitHub Pages is a good fit for this project because it is a **static site hosting service** that publishes HTML, CSS, and JavaScript from a GitHub repository, optionally through a build process. GitHub’s own documentation also makes the most important limitation explicit: GitHub Pages does **not** support server-side languages such as PHP, Ruby, or Python. That means the safest architecture is a purely static premium showcase with outbound platform links and, if needed, a lightweight third-party form endpoint for non-booking inquiries. citeturn12search13turn12search8

For implementation, use a modern static-site stack and deploy through **GitHub Actions**, which GitHub recommends when you use a custom build process or a generator other than default Jekyll. If the agents use a non-Jekyll static generator, the deployment should publish the built output folder to Pages and include a `.nojekyll` file if needed. GitHub’s documentation also confirms support for **custom domains** and HTTPS on correctly configured domains, which is important if you want this to feel like a real premium brand rather than a personal side project on a `github.io` URL. citeturn12search5turn12search8turn12search0turn12search3turn12search12

A clean repository structure would look like this:

```text
/
  public/
    images/
    video/
    icons/
  src/
    components/
    layouts/
    pages/
    content/
    styles/
    data/
  .github/
    workflows/
      deploy.yml
  README.md
  .nojekyll
```

Operationally, the Pages setup should include the following:

- a production branch and protected main branch;
- automatic preview deploys for pull requests if the team wants review links;
- one production workflow for build, test, and publish;
- environment variables only for non-sensitive analytics IDs, never for booking or payment flows;
- a custom domain with verified DNS and HTTPS once the design is stable. citeturn12search5turn12search15turn12search16

The outbound-booking design should be implemented as a **no-direct-booking architecture**. In practice that means no datepicker, no checkout, no on-site rate calculator pretending to be authoritative, and no payment collection. Instead, the site should have three classes of action:

- **Primary external actions:** “Check availability on Airbnb” and “Check availability on Vrbo.”
- **Secondary brand actions:** “Explore the villa,” “View gallery,” “See layout.”
- **Optional non-booking inquiry:** “Ask a question” or “Request the rate sheet.”

That structure is consistent with your brief and with the trust logic of keeping final booking on the established platforms. citeturn3view2turn1search16

Before launch, agents should run a final publication checklist: confirm the two platform links; confirm all room counts and stay-mode labels; confirm pool-cottage wording; confirm all policy notes; confirm the tax disclaimer; confirm mobile image cropping; confirm Lighthouse or equivalent performance targets; confirm accessibility basics; confirm analytics events on outbound clicks; and confirm that every image used on the site is owner-cleared. On a luxury showcase site, polish is not an extra. It is the product.

The finished result should feel like this: **a Riviera house with hotel-level taste, a magazine-level visual narrative, and the practical honesty of a trusted booking funnel**. That is the highest-leverage combination for this villa, this market, and this no-direct-booking constraint.
